Purpose

Why this portfolio exists

PHY101 is designed to build a rigorous analytical mindset by connecting conceptual physics with mathematical modelling and experimental checks. This ePortfolio is not a dump of notes. It is a curated trail of how I solved problems, where I got stuck, what the laboratory actually taught me, and how the five units talk to each other.

The module objective is to give a theoretical, quantitative, and practical framework for analysing complex physical phenomena — from calculus-based kinematics through Faraday’s law to the photoelectric effect. I organised the site the same way the work plan is organised: units, labs, assessment, then extra reading.

Units, notes, and worked classwork

Each card holds the idea in one sentence, a note from class, and a homework-style solution. Open the accordion for the full argument.

Class notes

In an inertial frame, Newton’s second law is F = dp/dt, not merely ma. With variable mass or momentum transfer that distinction matters. Friction and air resistance are not “errors” — they are force laws. Dry friction saturates at μN; linear drag is −bv; quadratic drag is −cv|v|. The work–energy theorem still holds: the work of every force equals ΔK, while only conservative forces can be stored as potential energy.

Impulse is the time-integral of force and equals Δp. In collisions I now ask two questions separately: is momentum conserved (external impulse ≈ 0)? is kinetic energy conserved (elastic)? Mixing those two questions was the most common homework mistake in the first fortnight.

Homework · kinematics with drag

A raindrop of mass m falls from rest under gravity with linear drag Fd = −bv. Find v(t) and the terminal speed.

Equation of motion: m dv/dt = mg − bv. Separate variables: dv / (mg − bv) = dt / m. Integrate from 0 to v and 0 to t:

v(t) = (mg/b) (1 − e−(b/m)t)

Terminal speed is the limit v∞ = mg/b. The exponential is not decoration: it is the signature that the unbalanced force shrinks as speed grows. If the drop were in vacuum, v = gt with no bound. Nature puts a bound in because air is a momentum reservoir.

Classwork · inelastic collision

A 0.25 kg dart at 12 m s−1 embeds in a 1.75 kg block at rest on a frictionless rail. Find the common speed and the fractional kinetic-energy loss.

Momentum: (0.25)(12) = (2.00) v ⇒ v = 1.5 m s−1. Initial K = 18 J; final K = 2.25 J; 87.5% of the kinetic energy is not “lost to nowhere” — it becomes internal energy (deformation, heat, sound). The work–energy theorem still accounts for it; mechanical energy is simply not conserved.

Class notes

A harmonic wave y(x,t) = A cos(kx − ωt + φ) is a solution of the one-dimensional wave equation ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²) ∂²y/∂t² with v = ω/k. Superposition is linear: if y1 and y2 are solutions, so is their sum. Beats are the audible consequence of two close frequencies — the envelope frequency is |f1 − f2|.

Intensity for a spherical wave falls as 1/r² because power is spread over area 4πr². Amplitude, not frequency, sets the energy carried by a mechanical wave of given impedance: average power P ∝ A² ω².

Drag the sliders. Constructive interference is not a special trick; it is what linearity looks like when two crests occupy the same place.

Class notes

Faraday’s law: ℰ = −dΦB/dt. The minus sign is Lenz’s law — induced current fights the change that produced it. Inductance stores energy U = ½LI² in the magnetic field, the dual of a capacitor’s ½CV². In matter, magnetisation modifies B through permeability; we must not treat vacuum formulae as universal.

Transient RC and RL circuits are first-order ODEs. The time constant τ = RC or L/R is the clock of the circuit. LC oscillation is the electromagnetic analogue of a mass-spring: energy sloshes between electric and magnetic forms at ω = 1/√(LC).

Homework · discharging capacitor

A 4.7 μF capacitor charged to 12 V discharges through 10 kΩ. Find τ and the current at t = τ.

τ = RC = 4.7×10−6 × 104 = 47 ms. I(t) = (V0/R) e−t/τ, so at t = τ, I = (1.2 mA)/e ≈ 0.44 mA. After one time constant the capacitor still holds 37% of its charge — “almost empty” is a laboratory myth. This same curve appears in Practical 4.

Class notes

Writing a wave as Ã ei(kx−ωt) turns interference into addition of phasors. Thin-film colours (soap, oil) are path-length plus a possible π phase shift on reflection from a higher-index surface. Single-slit minima sit at a sinθ = mλ; a grating’s principal maxima at d sinθ = mλ — the same geometry, opposite use (spread a spectrum vs. kill it).

Malus: I = I0 cos²θ. Brewster’s angle tan θB = n₂/n₁ produces fully polarised reflected light. Laser light is collimated, monochromatic, and temporally coherent; a lamp is none of those, even if it is bright.

Homework · soap film

A soap film (n = 1.33) in air looks bright in reflected 550 nm light at normal incidence. Find the smallest thickness.

Front surface: air→film, phase flip. Back surface: film→air, no flip. Constructive reflection then needs 2nt = λ/2 for the smallest t, so t = λ/(4n) ≈ 103 nm. The colour is not “in the soap”; it is a decision the wave makes after two journeys.

Class notes

Classical Rayleigh–Jeans radiation diverges in the ultraviolet; Planck’s E = nhf cuts the catastrophe. Einstein’s photoelectric equation Kmax = hf − φ explains why intensity never starts the effect below threshold frequency. Compton scattering Δλ = (h/mec)(1 − cosθ) treats the photon as a particle with momentum p = h/λ.

de Broglie reverses the arrow: λ = h/p for matter. The electron double-slit experiment is the least comfortable fact in the module — a single electron still builds an interference pattern. Heisenberg Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 is not a complaint about apparatus quality; it is a limit on what a wave packet can mean by “position” and “momentum” at once.

Homework · photoelectric threshold

Sodium has work function 2.28 eV. Find the threshold wavelength and Kmax for 400 nm light.

λ0 = hc/φ. With hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm, λ0 ≈ 544 nm. For 400 nm, hf = 3.10 eV, so Kmax = 0.82 eV. Bluer light raises stopping potential; brighter light only raises the number of electrons. That split is the experimental fingerprint of quantisation.

Personal insight

Reflections on learning

Units I & II — Mechanics and waves

I arrived treating kinematics as “suvat”. Calculus made the same problems feel unfamiliar until I saw that constant acceleration is just the special case a = dv/dt = constant. Friction stopped being a nuisance term when I had to decide whether energy or momentum was the conserved currency.

Waves corrected a school-level habit: I used to say “the particle travels with the wave.” Energy and phase travel; the air molecule mostly oscillates. Superposition clicked only after I drew two sinusoids and added them point by point — the interactive canvas above is the digital version of that notebook sketch.

Units III–V — Fields, light, quanta

Faraday’s minus sign felt pedantic until Lenz’s law saved a sign error in an inductance problem. Optics demanded comfort with complex exponentials; once a wave is a phasor, a diffraction integral is no longer a different subject from interference.

Quantum physics is where chemistry and this module meet. The photoelectric effect is why we talk about HOMO–LUMO gaps as energies, not as classical intensities. Heisenberg’s relation still sits uneasily with me: I can compute Δx Δp, but I am still learning to stop treating it as “the measurement disturbed the particle” and start treating it as “the particle was never a point with a sharp p”.

What I will do differently before the semester-end exam

I will derive, not memorise, the five laboratory working equations. I will keep a two-column error log: conceptual mix-ups (elastic vs inelastic, intensity vs amplitude) on one side, algebraic slips on the other. Independent study (45 of 120 hours) only works if I close the book and reconstruct the argument. This portfolio is that reconstruction, not a copy of the lecture.
